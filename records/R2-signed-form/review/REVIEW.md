## §1: signs, coefficients, and the form domain

I multiplied the literal Nambu matrices with symbolic real `r,Q,y` and differentiated in the trial amplitude, then averaged all four anchors. The anomalous coefficients of the four-`Q` product have pattern `+,-,+` at `a,b,c`; the normal coefficient is negative. The combination at anchor `p` is therefore `−Q_p Q_a Q_b Q_c y_p(y_p−y_a+y_b−y_c)`. The anchored alternating differences have relative signs `+,-,+,-`, giving the factor `−1/4`. For a `Q_p Q_v r_{v'}r_{v''}` monomial, exchanging the two ends preserves that coefficient and gives `(y_p−y_v)²/2`. Reanchors flip an increment or both increments; the kernel and measure are preserved. In the projected model, extend lines by zero; each term contains one line factor at every vertex, so the same changes of variables preserve the active parallelogram domain. All terms have real symmetric coefficients, so the real identity extends to the complex Hermitian form. The pairing coordinate has squared norm `∫uQ|y|²`; it is comparable to `Q0` by the width bounds of the branches used here. This equivalence is not a statement about arbitrary disconnected normal solutions.

The test at a single nonzero spatial transfer with zero bosonic index is **not** a zero-four-transfer test. Even when the alternating square is zero for the chosen trial, the real-line pair contribution need not be zero. For example, duplicate the two frequency lines (`p=a`, `b=c`) in the one-static momentum-independent bare limit; the exact symbolic answer is `Q_p Q_b r_p r_b (y_p−y_b)²`.

## §2: which quantity is held fixed?

The two equal-Fermi-point pairs out of four give `N(0) E_FS V₀=λ` with `q_c<π`. But the full-zone integral is `B₀=2λ v_F q_c`, not `2λΩ`. The second bound applies both to the normal Lipschitz constant and to the absolute symmetric pairing rows. Thus the exclusion is not just an estimate of the crossed term.

For an independent interpretation, use the standard Einstein convention `V₀(q)=2|g_q|²/Ω`. The bare zero-temperature one-loop frequency derivative at a Fermi point is `λ_m^(1)=∫ |g_q|²/(Ω+|ξ(k_F−q)|)² dq/(2π)`; the occupied and empty electron poles give the two signs of `ξ`, with the same squared denominator.
